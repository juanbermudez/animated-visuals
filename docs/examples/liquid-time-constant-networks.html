Example · a paper, explained

A neuron that speeds up when its input arrives

A visual guide to Ramin Hasani, Mathias Lechner, Alexander Amini, Daniela Rus and Radu Grosu, “Liquid Time-constant Networks”, AAAI 2021 · the paper. Built with Animated Visuals; every number from the paper says which equation, figure or table it comes from, and every simulated figure says so.

Most recurrent networks react at one speed, fixed once training ends. A liquid time-constant network (LTC) lets each neuron's speed depend on what it's hearing right now: fast when a signal arrives, slow when things go quiet. This guide follows one neuron reading one touch sensor, nothing and then a press, through the paper's equation, its two guarantees, its solver, and what all that buys on real data.

One neuron, one sensor

Picture a neuron wired to a touch sensor. While nobody touches it, the input sits near 0. Press it and the input jumps to 1. The neuron turns that into a smooth internal value, its state. How quickly the state follows is its time constant: roughly the seconds it takes to cover two thirds of the way to where it's heading.

In an LTC the time constant isn't a number you set. It comes out of the input. Drag the input knob at the right edge: press hard and the time constant drops toward 0.20 s, so the state snaps up; let go and it drifts back toward 0.93 s, so the state relaxes slowly.

The one change from older continuous-time networks

The paper starts from two older ideas. A neural ODE lets a small network f set how fast the state changes. A continuous-time RNN (CT-RNN) adds a leak, −x/τ, that pulls the state back to rest with one fixed time constant τ. The LTC changes where the input goes. It enters like a synapse on a real nerve cell: a current f·(A − x) that pulls the state toward a target A and grows with the input (Section 1, “Why this specific formulation?”). Multiply that out and you get the paper's Eq. 1:

dx/dt = −[ 1/τ + f(x, I, t, θ) ] · x + f(x, I, t, θ) · A

The input now sits inside the bracket that multiplies x, right beside the leak. That bracket sets how fast the state moves, so the time constant the system actually has is τsys = τ / (1 + τ·f). When f is large, τsys is small. Step through the same press for all three.

Bounded, however hard you push

A neuron whose speed follows its input sounds like it could run away. Section 4 proves it can't, with two theorems that only need the gate f to be a sigmoid between 0 and 1, scaled by a weight W.

Theorem 1 (Eq. 4): the time constant stays between τ/(1 + τW) and τ. Our neuron has τ = 1 s and W = 4, so it lives between 0.20 s and 1 s. Theorem 2 (Eq. 5): the state stays between min(0, A) and max(0, A). Both forces in Eq. 1 point into that range, the leak toward 0 and the gate toward A, so nothing can push the state out.

Feed the neuron a rough, made-up signal and turn its strength up to ×10. The time constant bangs against its floor, and the state never crosses its envelope. Drag the target A below zero and the envelope flips with it.

A CT-RNN with the same gate is bounded too, but by τ·W·A: its ceiling grows with the weights. The LTC's ceiling is A, whatever W is. The paper also proves LTCs can approximate any smooth dynamical system over a finite stretch of time (Theorem 3, Section 5), the same kind of guarantee CT-RNNs have.

A solver step that can't overshoot

Eq. 1 has no neat closed-form answer, so a computer steps it forward in small slices of time, Δt. The paper notes that LTCs are stiff: when the gate is strong the state wants to move fast, and a plain step that follows the current slope (explicit Euler) can shoot past the target. Runge-Kutta solvers cope by taking many tiny steps, which is slow (Section 2).

The paper's fused solver uses one trick. For the x that multiplies the bracket in Eq. 1, use the state at the end of the step instead of the start, and keep the start-of-step state inside f (Eq. 2). That makes the step solvable in one line, the paper's Eq. 3:

x(t + Δt) = [ x(t) + Δt · f · A ] / [ 1 + Δt · (1/τ + f) ]

Read it as a weighted average of three things: the current state, the target A and rest at 0. An average of numbers between 0 and A stays between 0 and A, so the step can't overshoot, however big Δt gets. Drag the step size on the time axis and compare.

In the paper's experiments each input sample is split into 6 of these fused steps (Algorithm 1 unfolds L steps; Section S5 sets the step to 1/6 of the input sampling period). Training runs plain backpropagation through all of those steps instead of the adjoint method neural ODEs often use: memory grows with the sequence, O(L × T) instead of O(1), but the gradients are accurate (Table 1, Section 3).

Longer paths from the same input

How do you measure whether one kind of network can draw more complicated shapes than another? The paper borrows trajectory length (Raghu et al. 2017): feed every network the same input, a circle traced once (I₁ = sin t, I₂ = cos t, for t from 0 to 2π), flatten its hidden state to 2D with PCA, and measure the length of the path it draws, like the ink a pen would use. The input circle itself has length 2π ≈ 6.28. A network that turns that circle into a long, tangled path is bending its input more (Section 5.1, Fig. 1).

The paper ran this on randomly initialised neural ODEs, CT-RNNs and LTCs, solved with RK45. The numbers below are printed in its Fig. 2. Pick a panel.

Theorems 4 and 5 (Eqs. 7–9) put lower bounds on how this length grows: for all three models it grows exponentially with depth times the number of solver steps, and the LTC's bound carries an extra factor that grows with the size of its hidden state. The paper's reading of its Figs. 2 and 3: the length grows linearly with width, the LTC's grows faster than linearly with the spread of the weights, and in multi-layer tanh and sigmoid networks none of the three grows with depth.

On real time series

Longer paths are a hint, not a result. Section 6 trains each model with 32 hidden units on real sequences, five runs each, and reports mean ± standard deviation (Tables 3, 4 and 6; setup in Section S5). Pick what to compare the LTC with; every row is drawn so that better is to the right.

The paper says LTCs improve on the others by 5% to 70% in four of seven Table 3 tasks. Against the best other model on each task the gains are smaller: 1.8% on gesture, 0.1% on occupancy, 41% less error on traffic and 6.3% on ozone. Against weaker baselines they're bigger (on gesture the LTC beats the neural ODE by 48%), so the range depends on which baseline you pick. On a second version of the person activity task (Table 5), set up to match Rubanova et al. 2019, the LTC scores 0.882 ± 0.005 against 0.846 ± 0.013 for the best latent ODE.

Where it is slower or weaker

The longer paths have a price. Table 2 counts how many solver steps each input sample takes on average, which the paper calls computational depth. The LTC needs far more than the others:

81.01solver steps per input for the LTC with Hard-tanh, against 0.61 for the neural ODE: 133× moreTable 2 · ± 10.05 and ± 0.02
56.9for the LTC with ReLU, against 1.29 for the neural ODE and 4.31 for the CT-RNNTable 2 · ReLU row
7.00for the LTC with sigmoid, against 0.56 for the neural ODE: the smallest gap, still 12.5×Table 2 · sigmoid row · ± 5.36

The paper says so plainly in Section 8: neural ODEs are “remarkably fast” next to LTCs, and the LTC's expressivity comes “at the expense of elevated time and memory complexity”. Two more limits from the same section:

  • Long-term dependencies. Like other continuous-time RNNs trained by gradient descent, LTCs suffer from vanishing gradients, so in their current form they're “not the obvious choice” for learning long-range dependencies.
  • The solver matters. They do well with variable-step solvers and the fused solver, but performance is “majorly influenced” when off-the-shelf explicit Euler is used: the overshoot in the solver figure.

And in Table 3 itself, three of seven tasks favour another model: activity recognition (neural ODE, 97.26% against 95.67%), sequential MNIST (LSTM, 98.41% against 97.57%) and power (CT-GRU, 0.586 against 0.642 squared error).
