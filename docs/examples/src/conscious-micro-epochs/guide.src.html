Conscious micro-epochs

A visual guide to Hesse, Lanfranchi, McGrory, Shi, Engel & Tsao, “Rapid alternation of conscious and suppressed stimulus representations in the primate brain”, bioRxiv preprint, October 2026 · the paper. Every number is from the paper and cites its figure.

Binocular flash suppression shows one face to each eye while only the flashed face is seen. Hesse et al. recorded macaque face cells during this task and found two alternating population states: state 1 encodes the flashed, seen face, and state 2 encodes the primed, suppressed face. Each state lasts about 60 ms, so averaging across time weakens both signals. This guide explains how the authors identified the states, what each state encodes and how they tested for sorting bias.

01 · The puzzle

Selectivity drops during rivalry

Take a cell that fires more for face A than face B when the two are shown one at a time. Its preference can be written as a modulation index, where R is the cell’s average firing rate:

Modulation indexMI = (RA − RB) / (RA + RB)

Now put the two faces in rivalry, one per eye. If the cell tracked what is seen, its index for the seen face should match its index when that face is shown alone. It does not. Across 5,550 cell–stimulus pairs in inferotemporal (IT) cortex, the index during rivalry was roughly a quarter of the index during plain viewing (slope 0.23, p = 8×10⁻¹³³). Earlier work had also shown the suppressed face is still decodable from IT.

Time-averaged IT activity therefore showed little selectivity for the seen face, even though the suppressed face remained decodable. This result bears on theories that place consciousness mainly in the front of the brain (global workspace and higher-order theories) or the back (integrated information and recurrent processing theories).

02 · The method

Binocular flash suppression

To pull apart what is seen from what is shown, the authors used binocular flash suppression. One face (the primed face) is shown to one eye for 1 to 2 seconds. Then a second face is flashed to the other eye. The flashed face takes over perception, and the primed face, still physically there, drops out of awareness.

They checked this in 14 people with button presses. The flashed face was reported on 82% of rivalry trials, close to the 87% people report for an image with no competitor at all (p = 4×10⁻⁷). Every neural analysis below uses the window from 300 to 1000 ms after the flash, when the flashed face dominates.

03 · The recording

The recordings

The authors recorded from two macaques with Neuropixels NHP probes. Each probe has a 45 mm shaft with 4,416 contacts, of which 384 record at a time. The recordings covered two face patches in inferotemporal cortex, ML and AM, and two prefrontal regions, PO (orbitofrontal) and PVL (ventrolateral). The patches were located with fMRI. One example trial recorded 429 cells together.

The monkeys saw 1,500 synthetic faces arranged in 750 rival pairs. Each face is a point in a 50-dimensional face space: 20 shape features and 30 appearance features. Face cells respond roughly as a straight line along one preferred direction in that space. That linear code is what lets the authors read a face back out of the spikes.

04 · The decoder

The decoder

First, the authors recorded each neuron’s average firing rate for each face when it was shown alone, during priming. That produced two templates per neuron: one for the flashed, seen face and one for the primed, suppressed face. During rivalry, they divided the spikes into 10 ms slices and tested which template more likely produced each slice. With Poisson spiking (spikes arriving at random at a set rate), a log-likelihood ratio scores which template fits better:

Log-likelihood ratio, one 10 ms sliceℓ(t) = Σi ni(t) · log(λci / λsi) − (λci − λsi) · Δt

Here ni is neuron i’s spike count in the slice, λc and λs are its template rates for the seen and suppressed face, and Δt is 10 ms. Each neuron contributes according to its face preference. A spike from a cell that strongly prefers the seen face raises ℓ; silence from that cell lowers it. Positive ℓ labels the slice state 1, the flashed, seen face. Negative ℓ labels it state 2, the primed, suppressed face.

In the recordings, during priming (one face on screen) most slices match that face. After the flash the population splits about evenly between the two states, trial after trial (Fig. 2c). For pairs where each face had its own preferring cells, the switching is visible by eye in single-cell rasters (Fig. 2e–g).

05 · Timing

How fast the states flip

If the labels were noise, the autocorrelation of ℓ would vanish within one 10 ms slice, as it does when the slices are shuffled. In the recordings, ℓ stays correlated for tens of milliseconds. Fitting a two-state switching model to that decay gives a mean dwell of 67 ms. A hidden Markov model, which allows each state to persist, gives median dwells of 64 ms for state 1 and 59 ms for state 2, stable across a range of bin sizes.

Each run of one state, a micro-epoch, lasts about 60 ms. Perception in classic binocular rivalry switches about once every two seconds (about 0.5 Hz). The population flips about 30 times faster, while the percept stays put.

06 · State content

What each state encodes

Because face cells encode faces linearly, a plain linear decoder trained on priming responses (each face shown alone) turns population activity into the 50 face features, and those into a picture. The authors applied that same decoder to rivalry activity three ways.

Applied to all moments, the decoder returns a blend that matches neither face. Applied only to state 1 moments, it returns the seen face. Applied only to state 2 moments, it returns the suppressed face, in detail, though the animal is not seeing it.

In state 1, some features of the seen face reached over 80% explained variance (how much of the true feature the reconstruction recovers), close to the over-90% ceiling set by priming. The suppressed face in state 1 sat at chance. State 2 reversed this (p = 3×10⁻²⁹).

07 · Controls

Controls against circular sorting

Section 06 has a circular sorting risk. The same spikes decide each slice’s state and then get averaged within that state. This can create an apparent difference without real switching: slices where a cell fired more are sorted one way, and slices where it fired less are sorted the other way. The authors call this circular conditioning bias. Their control lets some neurons decide the state and measures the others.

Tests with recorded data

  • Model comparison. A two-state model explained the activity better than the best single static state in all 19 sessions (p = 10⁻⁴).
  • Held-out neurons. States inferred from a random half of the neurons still made the other half reconstruct the seen face in state 1 and the suppressed face in state 2 (p = 6×10⁻¹⁷). Leaving out one neuron at a time did about 2 points better, so neuron count was not the limit.
  • Within-trial control. Forcing every trial to contribute equal state 1 and state 2 samples kept the reconstructions. The flips happen inside single trials.
  • Shuffled templates. With shuffled templates, reconstructions fell below zero explained variance.
  • Excitability control. Firing swings between high-gain On and low-gain Off periods. The state split was 49/51 during On and 50/50 during Off, agreed with a split by total firing no better than chance (Cohen’s κ ≈ 0), and a four-state decoder matched the two-state labels 80% of the time.

Tests with simulated populations

The authors simulated four kinds of population, each built from the real priming templates, and ran them through the same tests as the data. Only a population that truly switches as a whole passes everything, as the real data does.

08 · Single cells

One cell in both states

A face cell’s firing rises as a face moves along the cell’s preferred direction in face space. Order all the rival pairs by the seen face and the cell’s response rises in state 1 and stays flat in state 2. Order them by the suppressed face and the pattern reverses. The same cells change what they encode across the two states. Across 543 cells, gain for the seen face in state 1 and gain for the suppressed face in state 2 correlate at r = 0.90.

The two states are not mirror images. Tuning to the seen face in state 1 is 1.21× steeper than tuning to the suppressed face in state 2 (p = 3×10⁻⁴⁰). Mean firing is slightly higher in state 2 (p = 2×10⁻¹⁵). A linear decoder can tell a state 1 moment from a state 2 moment 66% of the time, against 50% chance.

09 · Selectivity

Why selectivity looked weak

The authors computed each cell’s selectivity separately in each state, with states inferred from the other half of the neurons. In state 1, cells behave as if the seen face were shown alone. In state 2, they behave as if the suppressed face were shown alone. Averaging the two produces the weak positive relationship measured in section 01.

The remaining positive slope comes from steeper tuning in state 1. Equalizing the gain of the two states before averaging removes that slope.

10 · Beyond IT

Prefrontal cortex

Prefrontal cortex does not hold a pure copy of the seen face. Decoding is weaker there overall, but the pattern is the same: state 1 gives the seen face, state 2 gives the suppressed face, and the split survives the held-out-neuron test.

The flips are only weakly coordinated between the two IT patches. States inferred in ML predicted AM’s content, and the reverse, but weakly (ML→AM p = 3×10⁻⁴, AM→ML p = 9×10⁻⁶). The authors say this is not strong evidence that the patches switch together, so each patch may operate partly independently.

11 · Conclusions

What the authors conclude

The authors conclude that conscious access requires temporal selection in addition to the regions involved.

Regional averages

Neither IT nor prefrontal averages hold a clean copy of what is seen. Averaging a region’s activity, front or back, removes the structure in time.

Suppressed-face representation

The suppressed face gets the same high-dimensional code as the seen one, in its own time slots, through the same neurons and the same feature mapping.

Timing

Similar alternation appears in rhythmic sampling in attention, brief bursts in working memory, alternating value states in orbitofrontal cortex, and flickering place maps in the hippocampus.

Unresolved routing

The data do not show why some epochs reach awareness. The two states are linearly separable, so the activity may carry a tag that determines where each epoch is sent.

Limits

  1. Preprint. Posted 16 October 2026 and not yet peer reviewed.
  2. Two monkeys, 19 sessions. Effects held in each animal and each session, but the sample of brains is two.
  3. The monkeys did not report what they saw. They fixated. Evidence that the flashed face was seen comes from 14 humans doing the same task.
  4. “Conscious state” means “looks like the flashed face”. The paper shows two representations alternating. The data cannot directly test whether each state 1 micro-epoch is experienced.
  5. In-sample reconstructions are partly inflated. They contain circularity bias; held-out reconstructions are lower, and the real data fall well short of the idealized two-state simulation. The authors suggest real transitions are gradual, not instant.
  6. Coordination across patches remains unresolved. If ML and AM flip on loosely coupled clocks, it is unclear how they produce one percept.
