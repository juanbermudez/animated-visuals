Face cells carry the seen face and the unseen face in turn, about 15 times a second

A visual guide to Hesse, Lanfranchi, McGrory, Shi, Engel & Tsao, “Rapid alternation of conscious and suppressed stimulus representations in the primate brain”, bioRxiv preprint, October 2026 · the paper. Show one face to one eye, then flash a second face to the other. The new face is seen; the first drops out of awareness, though it never leaves the screen. Recording hundreds of face cells at once in macaques, the authors found the population never settles on the seen face. About 15 times a second it flips between carrying the face that is seen and the face that is suppressed. Each run of one state is a micro-epoch, about 60 ms long. Averaged over time the two blur together, which is why a clean neural signal of conscious perception had never turned up.

01 · The puzzle

Face cells barely seem to care what is seen

Take a cell that fires more for face A than face B when the two are shown one at a time. Its preference can be written as a modulation index, where R is the cell’s average firing rate:

Modulation indexMI = (RA − RB) / (RA + RB)

Now put the two faces in rivalry, one per eye. If the cell simply tracked what is seen, its index for the seen face should match its index when that face is shown alone. It does not. Across 5,550 cell–stimulus pairs in inferotemporal (IT) cortex, the index during rivalry was roughly a quarter of the index during plain viewing (slope 0.23, p = 8×10⁻¹³³). Earlier work had also shown the suppressed face is still decodable from IT.

So high-level visual cortex looked like a muddy mix, never a clean copy of what is seen. That absence fed a long fight over where consciousness lives: the front of the brain (global workspace and higher-order theories) or the back (integrated information and recurrent processing theories).

02 · The trick

Keep the image fixed and let perception change

To pull apart what is seen from what is shown, the authors used binocular flash suppression. One face (the primed face) is shown to one eye for 1 to 2 seconds. Then a second face is flashed to the other eye. The flashed face takes over perception, and the primed face, still physically there, drops out of awareness.

They checked this in 14 people with button presses. The flashed face was reported on 82% of rivalry trials, close to the 87% people report for an image with no competitor at all (p = 4×10⁻⁷). Every neural analysis below uses the window from 300 to 1000 ms after the flash, when the flashed face dominates.

03 · The recording

Hundreds of neurons at once, in four places

Two macaques. Neuropixels NHP probes, each a 45 mm shaft with 4,416 contacts of which 384 record at a time, were lowered into two face patches of inferotemporal cortex, ML and AM, and two prefrontal regions, PO (orbitofrontal) and PVL (ventrolateral). The patches were found first with fMRI. In one example trial 429 cells were recorded together.

The monkeys saw 1,500 synthetic faces arranged in 750 rival pairs. Each face is a point in a 50-dimensional face space: 20 shape features and 30 appearance features. Face cells respond roughly as a straight line along one preferred direction in that space. That linear code is what lets the authors read a face back out of the spikes.

04 · The decoder

Every 10 ms, ask which face the spikes look like

First, record each neuron’s average firing rate for each face when it is shown alone, during priming. That gives two templates per neuron: one for the face that will be seen, one for the face that will be suppressed. Then, during rivalry, cut the spikes into 10 ms slices and ask which template more likely produced each slice. With Poisson spiking (spikes arriving at random at a set rate) the answer is a log-likelihood ratio, a score for which template fits better:

Log-likelihood ratio, one 10 ms sliceℓ(t) = Σi ni(t) · log(λci / λsi) − (λci − λsi) · Δt

Here ni is neuron i’s spike count in the slice, λc and λs are its template rates for the seen and suppressed face, and Δt is 10 ms. Each neuron casts a weighted vote. A spike from a cell that strongly prefers the seen face pushes ℓ up a lot; silence from that cell pulls it down a little. Positive ℓ labels the slice state 1 (looks like the seen face). Negative labels it state 2 (looks like the suppressed face).

In the recordings, during priming (one face on screen) most slices match that face. After the flash the population splits about evenly between the two states, trial after trial (Fig. 2c). For pairs where each face had its own preferring cells, the switching is visible by eye in single-cell rasters (Fig. 2e–g).

05 · The speed

The labels persist for tens of milliseconds, so the flips are real

If the labels were noise, ℓ would forget itself within one 10 ms slice, which is exactly what happens when the slices are shuffled. Instead ℓ stays correlated for tens of milliseconds. Fitting a two-state switching model to that decay gives a mean dwell of 67 ms. A hidden Markov model, which lets each state persist on purpose, gives median dwells of 64 ms for state 1 and 59 ms for state 2, stable across a range of bin sizes.

Each run of one state, a micro-epoch, lasts about 60 ms. Perception in classic binocular rivalry switches about once every two seconds (about 0.5 Hz). The population flips about 30 times faster, while the percept stays put.

06 · The content

Sort the moments, and two clean faces appear

Because face cells encode faces linearly, a plain linear decoder trained on priming responses (each face shown alone) turns population activity into the 50 face features, and those into a picture. The authors applied that same decoder to rivalry activity three ways.

Averaged over all moments, it returns a blend that matches neither face. Averaged over state 1 moments only, it returns the seen face. Averaged over state 2 moments only, it returns the suppressed face, in detail, though the animal is not seeing it.

In state 1, some features of the seen face reached over 80% explained variance (how much of the true feature the reconstruction recovers), close to the over-90% ceiling set by priming. The suppressed face in state 1 sat at chance. State 2 reversed this (p = 3×10⁻²⁹).

07 · The doubt

The sorting could fool itself, so the authors split the neurons

There is a trap in step 06. The same spikes decide each slice’s state and then get averaged within that state. That alone can fake switching. Slices where a cell happened to fire a little more get sorted one way, slices where it fired less get sorted the other way, and the two averages drift apart even if nothing switched. The authors call it circular conditioning bias. The fix is to let some neurons decide the state and measure the others.

Controls that held

  • Two states beat one. A two-state model explained the activity better than the best single static state in all 19 sessions (p = 10⁻⁴).
  • Split the neurons. States inferred from a random half of the neurons still made the other half reconstruct the seen face in state 1 and the suppressed face in state 2 (p = 6×10⁻¹⁷). Leaving out one neuron at a time did about 2 points better, so neuron count was not the limit.
  • Within trials, not between them. Forcing every trial to contribute equal state 1 and state 2 samples kept the reconstructions. The flips happen inside single trials.
  • The method cannot invent faces. With shuffled templates, reconstructions fell below zero explained variance.
  • Not just excitability. Firing swings between high-gain On and low-gain Off periods. The state split was 49/51 during On and 50/50 during Off, agreed with a split by total firing no better than chance (Cohen’s κ ≈ 0), and a four-state decoder matched the two-state labels 80% of the time.

Four simulated brains, four tests

The authors simulated four kinds of population, each built from the real priming templates, and ran them through the same tests as the data. Only a population that truly switches as a whole passes everything, as the real data does.

08 · The cells

The same neurons serve both faces, taking turns

A face cell ramps: its firing rises as a face moves along the cell’s preferred direction in face space. Order all the rival pairs by the seen face and the cell ramps in state 1 and goes flat in state 2. Order them by the suppressed face and it is the reverse. These are not two groups of cells, one per face. They are the same cells changing what they encode. Across 543 cells, gain for the seen face in state 1 and gain for the suppressed face in state 2 correlate at r = 0.90.

But the two states are not mirror images. Tuning to the seen face in state 1 is 1.21× steeper than tuning to the suppressed face in state 2 (p = 3×10⁻⁴⁰). Mean firing is slightly higher in state 2 (p = 2×10⁻¹⁵). A linear decoder can tell a state 1 moment from a state 2 moment 66% of the time, against 50% chance. Something in the activity marks which kind of epoch it is.

09 · The answer

The weak signal was an average of two strong ones

Back to the puzzle from step 01. Compute each cell’s selectivity separately in each state, with states inferred from the other half of the neurons. In state 1, cells behave as if the seen face were shown alone. In state 2, they behave as if the suppressed face were shown alone. Average the two and you get the weak, positive smear everyone found.

The leftover positive tilt comes from the conscious epochs tuning a bit more steeply. Equalize the gain of the two states before averaging and the tilt vanishes.

10 · Beyond IT

Prefrontal cortex flickers too

If consciousness were a front-of-brain affair, prefrontal cortex should hold a pure copy of the seen face. It does not. Decoding is weaker there overall, but the pattern is the same: state 1 gives the seen face, state 2 the suppressed one, and the split survives the held-out-neuron test.

The flips are also only weakly coordinated between the two IT patches. States inferred in ML predicted AM’s content, and the reverse, but weakly (ML→AM p = 3×10⁻⁴, AM→ML p = 9×10⁻⁶), and the authors say this is not strong evidence that the patches switch together. Each patch may run partly on its own clock.

11 · What it means

Consciousness may be a matter of when, not only where

The paper’s own conclusion: conscious access needs temporal selection, on top of whatever regions are involved.

No pure signal in any one place

Neither IT nor prefrontal averages hold a clean copy of what is seen. Searching for consciousness by averaging a region’s activity, front or back, misses the structure in time.

The unseen is not a faint trace

The suppressed face gets the same high-dimensional code as the seen one, in its own time slots, through the same neurons and the same feature mapping.

Time as a coding dimension

Similar flicker shows up elsewhere: rhythmic sampling in attention, brief bursts in working memory, alternating value states in orbitofrontal cortex, and flickering place maps in the hippocampus.

The open question

Why do some epochs reach awareness and others not? The two states are linearly separable, so the activity may carry a tag that decides where each epoch is sent.

Read with care

  1. Preprint. Posted 16 October 2026 and not yet peer reviewed.
  2. Two monkeys, 19 sessions. Effects held in each animal and each session, but the sample of brains is two.
  3. The monkeys never reported what they saw. They fixated. That the flashed face was seen rests on 14 humans doing the same task.
  4. “Conscious state” is a label for “looks like the flashed face”. The paper shows two representations alternating. That each state 1 micro-epoch is what gets experienced is an interpretation the data cannot test directly.
  5. The headline numbers are partly inflated. In-sample reconstructions carry the circularity bias; held-out ones are lower, and the real data fall well short of the idealized two-state simulation. The authors suggest real transitions are gradual, not instant.
  6. Unity is unexplained. If ML and AM flip on loosely coupled clocks, it is open how a single percept comes out of them.
