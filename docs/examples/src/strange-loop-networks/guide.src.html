Example · a paper, explained

Two language models that read each other's replies until one is satisfied

A visual guide to Francois Chaubard, “Strange-Loop Networks (SLNs): A novel class of ‘loopy’ Neural Network Architectures that mimic the cerebral cortex to natively encode self-reflection, hemispheric specialization, and an infinite composition of functions”, a self-published draft dated June 7, 2024, not peer-reviewed · the PDF. The claims are the author's; every number says where in the draft it appears.

Ask a language model for 2 + 2 or for 2534 × 5423 and it runs the same fixed stack of layers before the first token of either answer. Chaubard's draft proposes a fix borrowed from the two halves of the brain: seat a second model next to the first, let it read the answer, send its reaction back, and go round until it is satisfied. This guide follows one question, 2534 × 5423, around that loop. Up front: the draft is unfinished. It describes the architecture and six ways to train it, but its results table is empty.

What a strange loop is

The name comes from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979), which the draft quotes. In an ordinary hierarchy, levels stack: some text at the bottom, a judgment about the text above it, and nothing flows back down. A strange loop is a hierarchy where climbing up a level lands you back where you started, like Escher's two hands drawing each other. The draft builds that into a network: the judge's verdict about a draft becomes part of the text the writer reads next. Switch the wiring and watch the counter: the place repeats, the level keeps climbing.

Why two halves, according to the author

The motivation is an analogy with human evolution (Section 1.2, Figure 1). Brains grew for millions of years, then stopped, and the author argues the next gain came from specialization between the two hemispheres and a thicker corpus callosum linking them, not from more size. The draft lines this up with language models: they have been scaled up and up, and the next step should be two specialized models that talk. The sizes it gives:

450 cm³Australopithecines, roughly flat for over 6 million yearsSection 1.2
510 cm³Homo habilis, 2.1 to 1.5 million years agoSection 1.2
900 to 1,100 cm³Homo erectus, “doubled” from 1.9 million years agoSection 1.2
1,350 cm³Homo sapiens, from 300,000 years ago, unchanged sinceSection 1.2

The draft gives no source for these sizes, and the analogy is an argument, not evidence about networks. It also lists what it thinks one model can't do alone: second-guess itself before answering (it blames hallucinations on this), spend more thought on harder problems, reuse the same learned step many times, and improve by practising against itself (Section 1.1).

Two models, one loop

The definition is short. A strange-loop network is any architecture with at least two distinct models, a left one and a right one, where the left model's input is the right model's output and the other way round. They talk in what the author calls an inner dialogue loop until a stopping rule is met, and only then answer. The left model is the writer (the draft also calls it actor, generator or student); the right one is the judge (critic, reward model, discriminator or teacher). Around them sit an optional encoder and decoder, and a check that decides whether to go round again. Step through one question in the version the draft trains, where the judge only returns a score.

Three shapes of the same loop

The draft sketches three ways to build it. Tall: copy one pretrained model into both seats and steer each with its own system prompt. Wide: cut a model open, keep its first and last three blocks, and put a router in the middle that keeps choosing between two thin expert layers until it decides to answer. Lateralized: the tall version, but the right model only ever returns a score. The author calls the lateralized shape the most brain-like, citing brain scans that show the left hemisphere busier during logic tasks (no reference given), and it's the one the training sections focus on.

Hard questions earn more rounds

This is what the loop is for. A normal model has a fixed number of layers between your question and its first token, so 2 + 2 and 2534 × 5423 get the same budget (Section 1.1). In the loop, the stopping rule sets the budget: an easy prompt clears the bar in the first round, a hard one goes round until the judge is happy or a round limit is reached. Drag the bar.

Each round costs the writer one forward pass per token it writes, and the judge one pass, because in the lateralized shape it only returns a score (Section 5.2). Over several rounds that adds up: rounds × (draft tokens + 1). The draft writes the total for the general case as O(m1·m2·d), with m1 and m2 the two models' reply lengths and d the rounds, calls it expensive next to one model, and says that is “more than offset” by needing fewer layers and a smaller model (Section 5). It doesn't measure either side.

Training: the writer chases the judge's score

Running the loop is the easy part. Most of the draft is about training the two models together, ideally without paying people to rank answers (Section 5). It reviews six recipes and writes three out as algorithms. The one it develops furthest is actor-critic, Algorithm 2: the writer is trained to raise the judge's score, and the judge is trained to tell the true answer from the writer's drafts. That second half has a sharp edge, which the last steps show with numbers.

Pavlovian gradient decay: go easy on early drafts

Training the judge is lopsided: each prompt has one true answer and as many wrong drafts as you care to sample. The author's fix, named Pavlovian gradient decay (Section 5.5), turns one example into many. Every prefix of the true answer becomes a positive and the writer's draft from every round becomes a negative, each with a value from the curve Pavlovian conditioning uses for how learning fades with delay, C(ΔT) = C₀ · e−λΔT (Equation 9). Here ΔT counts rounds back from the end instead of seconds. The full true answer gets all of C₀ and the last draft gets nothing; the rest fall in between. The intent is to be lenient with early drafts, because the writer is supposed to need a few rounds of thinking. Drag λ.

Where the curve comes from: in conditioning, the draft says, typical λ in humans is 0.1 to 0.5, with the best learning at 1 to 4 seconds between stimulus and reward (Section 5.5, no source given). Those two values are marked on the slider. Nothing in the draft says what λ should be for a network.

Why not train straight through the loop

The obvious move is to treat the loop as one big network and backpropagate through every round. Sampling a token isn't differentiable, so the author replaces each sampled token with a “soft token”, a mix of word embeddings at a temperature of 0.001, close enough to one word that the judge can read it (Equations 3 and 4). The draft says this converges. The catch is memory: the backward pass needs every activation from every token of every round. On Pythia-70M at a batch size of 1 the author measured 3.9 GB of GPU memory, which the draft calls a 30× increase over the model's size, and projects 240 GB for an 8-billion-weight model (Section 5.1). Keeping only the last round, truncated backpropagation, would fit, but the author rejects it because only the last round would learn.

What the draft has not shown yet

Here's the uncomfortable part. The abstract promises “remarkable improvements on many common benchmarks of XX%”, and the list of contributions ends with “achieves state-of-the-art results on many benchmarks. [NEED TO GO PROVE!]”. Section 6, Results, reads “TODO :)”. The comparison it plans, seven setups labelled A to G from a single model up to the lateralized wide loop (Section 1.5), isn't run. The only evidence it cites for loops of models is other people's: prompting methods where one model reflects on its own output, Reflexion and Self-Refine, which it says gained 10 to 15% on HumanEval and MBPP (Section 1.3, no reference given). The draft says code, demos and datasets are released at github.com/fchaubard/; this guide didn't check.

What to take from it

As a definition, the draft is clear: two models, each reading the other's last reply, with a rule for when to stop. As a design space it's generous: three shapes, six training recipes, one named trick for the judge's data. As evidence, it has nothing yet, and its own placeholders say so. Read it as a research plan from June 2024, not a result. The open problems are the ones the author names: training through the loop doesn't fit in memory, the alternatives either need human preference data or risk a writer that overfits its judge, and nothing has been measured against a single model.
