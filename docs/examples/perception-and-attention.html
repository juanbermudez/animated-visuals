Example · an open-source guide, in 2D

How your senses reach the brain, and how attention picks among them

A 2D visual guide to Perception & Attention, Juan Bermudez's open-source review of human perception and attention on a 3D brain (MIT). Every fact and number comes from its six walkthroughs and the papers they cite. Built with Animated Visuals.

You're looking for a friend in a crowd. Faces, voices, a hand on yours: it all arrives at once, and the brain can't process all of it in detail. This guide follows each sense from the body to the cortex, then shows the two ways the brain controls its own input: the cortex adjusting its relays, and attention choosing what to favour. The last figure is the original's small attention model, which you can push yourself.

Vision: split, crossed and relayed before the cortex sees it

Your eyes scan the crowd. In the retina, rods and cones absorb light and local circuits compare neighbouring points, so what leaves the eye mostly reports contrast and change. At the optic chiasm the fibres are regrouped: everything in the right half of what you see goes to the left hemisphere, from both eyes. The LGN in the thalamus relays the signal to V1, and two streams leave from there: one to recognize your friend's face, one to reach out and wave.

The classic evidence for two streams is a patient known as D.F. With damage to her ventral stream, she could not report how a slot was oriented, yet could post a card through it accurately. Goodale and Milner concluded that her intact dorsal stream guided the movement; critics argue the streams are less independent than this case suggests. One more detail the steps skip: in the LGN of anaesthetised monkeys, the fastest motion-channel responses came about 10 ms before the fastest detail-channel ones.

Touch: two routes that cross at different levels

Your friend grabs your right hand. That touch rises on the right side of the spinal cord and only crosses over in the medulla; then the thalamus relays it to S1, the body map in your left hemisphere. If someone steps on your foot at the same moment, the pain takes a different road: it crosses straight away, in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, and reaches the posterior insula and the cingulate cortex as well as S1 and S2.

Surgeons rely on that separation. Destroying the pain route with a heat probe high in the neck, on the side opposite the pain, relieves severe one-sided cancer pain: in a study of 19 such patients, pain, temperature and itch on the painful side were lost or greatly reduced, while the threshold for detecting light touch did not change. Whether any cortical area responds to pain alone is still debated.

Hearing: the two ears meet in the brainstem

Someone calls your name from your left. The sound reaches your left ear a fraction earlier, and a little louder, because your head blocks some of it from the right. Each cochlea sorts it by frequency, and the two ears first meet in the superior olive, which compares those differences to place the voice. From there the route climbs through the inferior colliculus and the MGN to auditory cortex, where a front stream helps work out what the sound is and a back stream where it came from.

The route also runs backwards. Feedback fibres from the olive reduce the cochlea's amplification of quiet and moderate sounds; in animals this helps the auditory nerve signal brief sounds in background noise and protects the ear from loud noise. Whether attention does this in people is open: one study measured sounds emitted by the ear, and in the first 15 listeners they were 5.4 dB weaker during visual attention than during auditory attention, while the next 15 showed no effect.

Speech: one stream for meaning, one for saying it

You shout your friend's name back. The word you heard reaches auditory cortex and the superior temporal gyrus. A ventral stream links the sounds to meaning, in both hemispheres; a dorsal stream, mainly on the left, maps them through area Spt and Broca's area to the motor cortex that moves your lips and tongue. In normal speech these regions are active at the same time and exchange signals both ways; the steps follow the order of repeating a word.

Broca's area is less settled than its fame suggests. Some groups place speech planning there; others point to the middle precentral gyrus, higher up and further back. In a study of 134 stroke survivors, damage to Broca's area did not predict lasting speech problems; damage to the white matter deeper in, above the insula, did.

The cortex tunes its own input

Here is the surprising part: the thalamus is not a passive relay. Of the synapses on one LGN relay cell in cats, only about 7% come from the eye, and about 30% come from layer 6 of the cortex, the very place the relay sends its output. That feedback doesn't change what the cell responds to; it changes how strongly. Through the TRN, a thin shell of inhibitory cells, the same feedback can also turn the relay down. A second output, from layer 5, drives the pulvinar, which carries signals from one cortical area to another beside the direct connections.

Attention uses this gate. In monkeys, attention raises LGN responses and lowers TRN responses, and the same thalamic circuit produces sleep spindles, a brain rhythm of light (non-REM) sleep. The caveat, from the original: the switch between turning the relay down and up was shown in the mouse touch thalamus, not measured in human vision, and in cats and monkeys the evidence for the layer 5 route is mainly anatomical.

Attention: many areas set priorities

Back to the crowd. You're holding a goal, find your friend, in prefrontal cortex, which with the frontal eye fields and parietal cortex favours the features and places that match it. Then someone shouts behind you, and the temporoparietal junction (TPJ) and the salience network pull attention toward the surprise. Underneath, the locus coeruleus affects how alert you are, and the superior colliculus and pulvinar help select and coordinate. It all acts on sensory cortex, where responses to what you attend go up and the rest go down.

Unattended signals are weakened, not erased: they are still processed and can still draw attention. That is what the last figure is about.

Why favouring one sense dims the others

The original guide models this with one equation, a simplified form of the normalization model of attention (Reynolds and Heeger, 2009). Each sense's response is its input times its attention gain, divided by a shared total: R = A·E / (σ + Σ A·E), with σ = 1. Give one sense more gain and the shared total grows, so every other sense's share shrinks. Nothing is switched off.

Searching the crowd, you give vision priority. At a gain of , response rises from to , and each other sense falls to : lower, but not zero. Drag the number, or the dot in the figure.

Two limits, both stated in the original. The published model describes competition between stimuli inside visual cortex, where the total is weighted by distance in space and feature; pooling three senses in one total is an illustration. In human imaging, attending to one sense does raise activity in its sensory cortex and lower it in the others, but it is not established that normalization causes this. For scale: in monkeys with a single stimulus in view, attention raised responses by about 26% in V4 and 8% in V1, and effects are larger when several stimuli compete.

Checked against the source

The source is the original guide's content (src/content/pathways.ts, region-guides.ts, sources.ts), its model (src/model/attention.ts) and its fact-check notes (docs/science-factcheck.md), at commit 8fd7194. Each number below is from the step named, with the paper the original cites for it.

  • Vision. 96 million photoreceptors, about 1 million ganglion cells (0.7 to 1.5 million): step “Light becomes neural signals” (Curcio et al. 1990; Curcio & Allen 1990). About half the fibres cross, 53% from one count: “Fibres cross at the optic chiasm” (a single 78-year-old subject, per the fact-check). About 80% and 10% of ganglion cells to the parvocellular and magnocellular layers, about 10 ms earlier magnocellular responses (monkeys): “Parallel channels in the LGN” (Perry et al. 1984; Maunsell et al. 1999). The inverted V1 map: “Primary visual cortex”. D.F.: “Two streams leave V1” (Goodale & Milner 1992; critique, Schenk & McIntosh 2010). Frozen tea: “Motion in area MT”. Optic ataxia: “Location and action”. About 100 IT neurons over 12.5 ms (monkeys): “Object recognition” (Hung et al. 2005). Distorted faces: “Areas for faces, places, bodies and words”.
  • Touch. 35–75 m/s and 0.4–1.4 m/s: “Touch and position: up the dorsal columns”. Right hand in the left thalamus: “Relay in the VPL”. Penfield: “Primary somatosensory cortex”. S1 and S2 removals (macaques): “Secondary somatosensory cortex”. Cordotomy in 19 patients: “Pain and temperature cross in the spinal cord” (Marshall et al. 2019). 4,160 stimulations in 164 patients, 1.4% painful: “Posterior insula” (Mazzola et al. 2012). Hypnosis: “Cingulate cortex” (Rainville et al. 1997).
  • Hearing. About 3,500 inner hair cells: “Sound is sorted by frequency”. About 10 microseconds: “Comparing the two ears”. Eight proposed belt areas (monkeys): “Belt and parabelt” (Kaas & Hackett 2000). 15 patients, four and three: “Two streams leave auditory cortex” (Clarke et al. 2002). Up to 4 dB in chinchillas; 5.4 dB in the first 15 listeners and no effect in the next 15: “Feedback to the ear” (Delano et al. 2007; Beim et al. 2018).
  • Speech. Seven patients: “Planning speech” (Flinker et al. 2015). 134 stroke survivors: same step (Gajardo-Vidal et al. 2021). 0.2 s delay, 17 speakers, two to three times as often: “Controlling articulation” (Stuart et al. 2002).
  • Cortex–thalamus feedback. 7% retinal and about 30% each from cortex, brainstem and inhibitory cells (cats): “Input to the LGN” and “Feedback from layer 6” (Van Horn, Erişir & Sherman 2000; Erişir et al. 1997). The original gives the three as “about 30%”; the bar draws them equal at 31% so it sums to 100. Slow activity reduces, about 10 pulses per second increases (mouse slices), and the 2024 awake-mouse study: “Inhibition through the TRN” (Crandall et al. 2015; Dimwamwa et al. 2024). Whisker texture task (mice): “A second route through the thalamus” (Mo, McKinnon & Sherman 2024).
  • Attention. Activity before anything appears: “Holding a goal” (Kastner et al. 1999). FEF stimulation improving attention at the matching location only (monkeys): “Steering the eyes and attention” (Moore & Fallah 2001; its effect on V4, Moore & Armstrong 2003). About 90 ms and roughly 200 ms (monkeys): “Arousal and neuromodulators” (Aston-Jones et al. 1994, per the fact-check). Superior colliculus silencing (monkeys): “Priority map in the midbrain” (Lovejoy & Krauzlis 2010). Pulvinar synchrony (monkeys): “Thalamic coordination” (Saalmann et al. 2012). About 26% in V4 and 8% in V1 (monkeys, medians): “Effects in sensory cortex” (McAdams & Maunsell 1999).
  • The model (Fig. 7). R = A·E / (σ + Σ A·E), σ = 1, gain from 1× to 3× for the sense with priority and 1× for the others, E = 1 for a sense that is shown: src/model/attention.ts, after Reynolds & Heeger 2009. The default gain of 2.3× is the original's default (its slider at 65 of 100). Every response, total and division in the figure and the prose is computed from that equation; none is typed by hand. With all three senses shown and no priority, each response is 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25; at 3× it is 3 ÷ 6 = 0.50 against 1 ÷ 6 ≈ 0.17.
  • Simulated or illustrative. Every figure's layout is schematic, not anatomical. Packet speed and count, line thickness, the layer depths in Fig. 5 and the packet rates of the slow and 10-per-second steps are illustrative. Fig. 7's numbers come from a simplified model, not measurements, and pooling senses in one total is the original's illustration.
  • Left out on purpose. The 3D brain, the atlas geometry and everything about where regions sit; the region guides beyond one-line tips; routes the original also leaves out (the superior colliculus and pretectum in vision, the trigeminal route for the face, lateral olivocochlear fibres); the original's guide tools, notes and quizzes. Fig. 4 shows the left hemisphere only. Fig. 6 draws the route from the cingulate cortex to the locus coeruleus and the noradrenaline routes, but not acetylcholine from the basal forebrain, dopamine, the basal ganglia, memory systems or the default mode network, which the original also omits.
